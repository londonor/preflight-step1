# Gate 1 · Bioengineered meniscus

**Verdict — Advance.** A real, recognized, counted problem on the desk evidence, with one large question routed to the interviews and to gate 3: how many patients actually live with it. The five conversations in the discovery kit are the next step, not an option.

2026-11-09 · autonomous run · origin: founder request, 9 Nov 2026 — first run of a new idea under the current skill

---

## At a glance

- **The problem** — When a torn meniscus cannot be repaired, the tissue is removed, and the knee that loses it develops arthritis and comes to replacement at many times the rate of an intact knee; the only tissue replacement in use — a donor meniscus — reaches a tiny fraction of patients, is sized with error, and survives in about three of four patients at ten years and six of ten at fifteen.
- **Where to start** — patients under about 55 with a symptomatic meniscus-deficient knee after subtotal or total meniscectomy: the population the field already treats with meniscal allograft transplantation (MAT).
- **The numbers** — 13.49% of 120,493 patients had a knee replacement within 15 years of a partial meniscectomy, 10 times the general population and 40 times for those aged 30–39 (UK national cohort, 2019); 48% of open-meniscectomy knees had radiographic arthritis at 21 years vs 7% of matched controls (1998); MAT survives in 73.5% at 10 years and 60.3% at 15 (systematic review, 2019); MAT is performed at 0.12–0.24 per 100,000 people per year in the US (2015, via a 2026 review).
- **What the desk could not find** — how many patients have a symptomatic meniscus-deficient knee · why MAT is so rare · what the problem costs (allograft, MAT episode, downstream arthroplasty) · how often a young patient's tear is irreparable · a US arthroplasty-after-meniscectomy rate.
- **Next step** — five independent conversations, built around the eight gap questions below.

## The problem

**Problem sentence** · agent-resolved — the input named only the technology; restated from the field's own sources

> When a torn meniscus cannot be repaired, the tissue is removed, and the knee that loses it develops arthritis and comes to replacement at many times the rate of an intact knee. The only tissue replacement in use — a donor meniscus — reaches a tiny fraction of patients, is sized with error, and survives in about three of four patients at ten years and six of ten at fifteen.

Sources: Abram, *Bone Joint J* 2019 · Roos, *Arthritis Rheum* 1998 · Novaretti, *Arthroscopy* 2019 · Araujo-Espinoza, *Ann Jt* 2026 · Beaufils, *KSSTA* 2017

**Proposed technology** · founder-stated — recorded for later gates, not evaluated here

> "Bioengineered meniscus."

**First indication** · agent-selected from the candidates below

> Patients under about 55 with a symptomatic meniscus-deficient knee after subtotal or total meniscectomy — today's candidates for meniscal allograft transplantation.

### Candidate indications considered

**1 · Symptomatic meniscal deficiency after subtotal or total meniscectomy, under about 55 (the MAT population) — selected**

- Recognized by: Araujo-Espinoza, *Ann Jt* 2026 ("MAT is primarily indicated for symptomatic meniscal deficiency in patients younger than 55 years of age"); Gitelis, *Am J Orthop* 2018 (ideal candidate "chronologically and physiologically young patient (<50 years) with symptomatic meniscal deficiency")
- Number: the current treatment survives in 73.5% at 10 years and 60.3% at 15 (Novaretti 2019, 658 patients); complication rate 21.3% and failure rate 10.6% in a 1,068-patient review (Elattar, via Gitelis 2018); performed at 0.12–0.24 per 100,000 persons per year in the US, 2007–2011 (Cvetanovich 2015, via the 2026 review)
- Acuteness: high — young, symptomatic, on a path to arthroplasty; the field calls the treatment "underutilized" and says "graft deterioration inevitably occurs at 7 to 10 years." Weakness: the population is small as measured, and no denominator was found

**2 · Patients undergoing arthroscopic partial meniscectomy (replace at the time of removal) — not selected**

- Recognized by: 2016 ESSKA consensus (patients after APM for degenerative lesions "present a higher risk for symptomatic knee osteoarthritis," Grade C); Abram 2019
- Number: 13.49% knee arthroplasty within 15 years; RR 10.27 vs the general population; RR 39.62 at ages 30–39; HR 2.99 against the patient's own other knee (Abram 2019, n = 834,393, mean age 50); 387,833 meniscectomies in 2005–2011 in a database covering about 9% of the US population under 65 (Abrams 2013); "approximately 700,000 meniscectomies are performed annually" in the US (Gitelis 2018, secondary)
- Acuteness: large and well counted, but the harm arrives 10–20 years later, most patients are around 50 with degenerative tears for whom the consensus answer is not to operate first, and the field is already shifting from removal to repair. The young subgroup (RR 39.62 at 30–39) is the strongest alternative first indication

**3 · Total or open meniscectomy — not selected**

- Recognized by: Roos 1998 (open meniscectomy, 1973 cohort)
- Number: radiographic arthritis KL ≥ 2 in 48% at 21 years vs 7% of matched controls; RR 14.0 (95% CI 3.5–121.2)
- Acuteness: the clearest harm number in the record, but for a procedure no longer performed as such; it serves as evidence of seriousness, not as a place to start

## The seven questions

*Answered from the desk. Each resolves to Yes, No, or Open.*

### 1 · Can the problem be stated without the technology?

**Yes.** The problem sentence above does; the ESSKA consensus and the MAT reviews state it in the same terms.

### 2 · Who has the problem?

**Yes** — and the person who removes the tissue, the person who treats the consequence, and the person who pays for it are three different people, often a decade apart.

- **Users** — orthopaedic sports-medicine surgeons who perform MAT at specialized centers; the much larger group of orthopaedic surgeons who perform meniscectomy and repair and refer out for MAT
- **Patients** — young and middle-aged adults with a meniscus-deficient knee: pain and activity limits now, arthritis and arthroplasty later
- **Purchasers** — hospitals and surgery centers; donor menisci are supplied by musculoskeletal tissue banks
- **Payers** — insurers and Medicare, which fund the MAT episode now and the arthroplasty 10–20 years later; the arthroplasty surgeon inherits the knee

### 3 · How often, and how bad?

**Yes** — verified numbers for the harm of meniscal loss and for the performance of today's replacement; the size of the affected population was not found.

- **Harm of losing meniscal tissue** — 13.49% (16,256 of 120,493) knee arthroplasty within 15 years of partial meniscectomy; RR 10.27 vs the general population; RR 39.62 at ages 30–39; HR 2.99 for the operated knee vs the patient's own other knee (Abram 2019, UK, n = 834,393, mean age 50). After open meniscectomy, radiographic arthritis KL ≥ 2 in 48% at 21 years vs 7% in matched controls, RR 14.0; knee symptoms about twice as common (Roos 1998, n = 107)
- **Performance of today's replacement** — MAT survivorship 73.5% at 10 years and 60.3% at 15 years; two studies at 19 and 24 years, 50% and 15.1% (Novaretti 2019, 11 studies, 658 patients). Complication rate 21.3%, failure 10.6% (Elattar, 44 studies, 1,068 patients, via Gitelis 2018; no timeframe given). Graft failure within 5 years 1.8–25% and reoperation 1.6–33% depending on fixation technique (Araujo-Espinoza 2026)
- **How often** — MAT at 0.12–0.24 per 100,000 persons per year in the US, 2007–2011, with "only marginal increases over the past decade" (Cvetanovich 2015, via the 2026 review). Meniscectomies: 387,833 in 2005–2011 in a database covering about 9% of the US under-65 population, with no significant increase over the period (Abrams 2013); "approximately 700,000 … annually" (Gitelis 2018, secondary)
- **Workaround burden** — a donor organ with sizing error (7.8% average by the standard radiographic method; anthropometric methods "often exceed the 10% error threshold deemed clinically acceptable"), processing trade-offs (irradiation "associated with diminished mechanical strength"; fresh-frozen grafts "limited cellular viability"), and a concurrent osteotomy when the leg is malaligned (Araujo-Espinoza 2026)
- **Cost** — not found: no cost per MAT episode, allograft price, or arthroplasty cost attributable to meniscal loss was opened
- **Not found** — the number of patients with a symptomatic meniscus-deficient knee (the denominator); how often a young patient's tear is irreparable

### 4 · How is it solved today?

**Yes.** Repair when the tear allows it, and increasingly so; partial meniscectomy when it does not; for the symptomatic meniscus-deficient knee in a young patient, meniscal allograft transplantation, with a high tibial osteotomy when the leg is malaligned; partial synthetic scaffolds in some markets, only when a functional meniscal rim remains; otherwise non-operative care until the knee reaches arthroplasty.

### 5 · What is wrong with today's solution, in the field's own words?

**Yes** — the field says removal harms the knee, the replacement is scarce and wears out, and the partial substitutes have not shown an advantage.

- **ESSKA 2016 consensus** — "Loss of meniscus function may negatively affect the knee in the long term" (Grade B); "the high risk of osteoarthritis after APM remains a concern"; total meniscectomy carries a higher risk than partial, and the lateral side higher than the medial (Grade C)
- **On MAT** — it "remains underutilized in clinical practice"; "graft deterioration inevitably occurs at 7 to 10 years"; "the decision often depends on the surgeon's preference and graft availability"; donor–recipient sex mismatch is "associated with inferior graft survivorship" (Araujo-Espinoza 2026; Gitelis 2018)
- **On partial scaffolds** — "Neither CMI nor Actifit has demonstrated a clear advantage over partial meniscectomy"; "the long-term chondroprotective effect of these treatments is still unclear"; "no studies have evaluated meniscal scaffold outcomes relative to the MCID or longitudinal osteoarthritis progression" (Araujo-Espinoza 2026)
- **The trade-off** — remove tissue and accept the arthritis risk, or transplant a donor meniscus that is hard to size, processed at a cost to its strength or cells, and expected to deteriorate within a decade

### 6 · Is anyone looking to change?

**Yes** — with a flag for gate 8.

- **Demand** — a "save the meniscus" shift in practice: repairs doubled from 2005 to 2011 while meniscectomies were flat (Abrams 2013); debridement fell and repair rose in every age group from 2001 to 2017 (ABOS case list, 2021); a European society consensus (2016) moved surgery off the first line for degenerative tears; two meniscus implants hold FDA Breakthrough Device designations and one plans a De Novo submission in 2026 (BONEZONE, Feb 2026) — recorded as demand evidence, not evaluated
- **Practice-shift check** — the same shift shrinks the problem at its source: fewer meniscectomies for degenerative tears and more repairs mean fewer meniscus-deficient knees over time. Nothing in the record says the irreparable tear is disappearing, but the population should be assumed to be contracting at the margin. Flag gate 8

### 7 · Any obvious contradiction with a business?

**No** — but a timing split worth recording. The surgeon who removes the tissue does not see the arthritis; the arthroplasty surgeon who sees it did not remove the tissue; the payer who funds the replacement at 50 may not be the payer who funded the meniscectomy at 35. Who would pay for a replacement at the time of loss is gate 6.

## The case against

*The strongest honest argument that the problem is not real, not consequential, already solved, or owned by no one.*

- The most dramatic harm number — 48% arthritis vs 7%, RR 14 — comes from open meniscectomy performed in 1973, a procedure nobody does now. The modern cohort shows 13.49% arthroplasty at 15 years in patients with a mean age of 50 and degenerative tears, and ESSKA itself calls the degenerative tear "a feature of a knee at increased risk of developing osteoarthritis": the knee, not the meniscectomy, may be the problem. The within-patient comparison (HR 2.99 against the other knee) blunts this but does not settle it.
- The acute population — the young, symptomatic, meniscus-deficient knee — is tiny as measured: MAT at 0.12–0.24 per 100,000, flat for a decade. Either the problem is rare, or the field has decided to live with it; "underutilized" may mean surgeons do not see enough need to refer.
- The field already has a tissue replacement, and its main response to the problem is upstream: repair more, remove less. That shrinks the population a replacement would serve.
- Most meniscectomy patients are over 50 with degenerative tears, and for them the consensus answer is "do not operate first," not "replace what you removed."
- Nobody has priced the problem. No cost per MAT, allograft, or attributable arthroplasty was found, so its economic weight is unmeasured.

---

## Verdict

**Advance.** Losing meniscal tissue is a real, recognized, counted harm — a tenfold arthroplasty risk in a national cohort of 834,393, fortyfold in the young — and the only tissue replacement the field has is rare, hard to size, and expected to deteriorate within a decade, by the field's own account. Today's standard of care does not solve it, and the field's response has been to remove less rather than replace better. The open question is not whether the problem is real but how many people have the acute form of it; that goes to the interviews and to gate 3. The harm is arthritis and reoperation, not death — recorded for the founder.

## Customer-discovery kit

*Five independent conversations with people who have the problem. The gap questions come first because they are what the desk could not learn.*

### The sentence to test

> "When a young patient's meniscus can't be repaired you take it out, and you know what that knee looks like in fifteen years — and the only tissue you can put back is a donor meniscus that's hard to size and wears out in a decade."

### Who to talk to

Five people, independent of each other and of the founder:

1. A sports-medicine orthopaedic surgeon who performs meniscal allograft transplantation at a center that does them regularly
2. An orthopaedic surgeon who performs meniscectomy and repair but refers MAT out — the person at the entry point, who decides what to remove and whom to refer
3. An arthroplasty surgeon who replaces knees in patients with a meniscectomy in their history — the person who inherits the consequence
4. Someone at a musculoskeletal tissue bank who fills meniscal allograft orders — the person who lives with sizing, supply, and processing
5. The person who pays — a payer medical director who approves MAT, or the orthopaedic service-line administrator who absorbs the allograft and the episode cost

If a sixth conversation is possible: a patient who had a meniscectomy young and a MAT or a knee replacement later.

### Gap-targeted questions

*What the desk could not find, and the question that would find it.*

#### Gap 1 · How many patients have a symptomatic meniscus-deficient knee

*Not found.* The harm of loss is counted; the number of people living with it is not. The desk found how many get the treatment (0.12–0.24 per 100,000), not how many need it.

- **Ask** — "In a typical year, how many patients do you see whose knee pain you attribute to missing meniscus? How many of those do you offer a transplant, and what happens to the rest?"
- **Closed if** — surgeons give a count per year and describe the ones who are not offered MAT and why
- **Warning if** — "a handful, if that" from the MAT surgeon and "I can't think of one" from the general surgeon

#### Gap 2 · Why MAT is so rare

*Practitioner-only.* The field calls MAT "underutilized" without saying why: graft availability, sizing, cost, training, patient reluctance, or lack of need.

- **Ask** — "The last patient you thought should have a meniscal transplant and didn't get one — what stopped it?"
- **Closed if** — they name a specific obstacle (waiting for a size match, the payer, the osteotomy, the patient) and can say how often it recurs
- **Warning if** — "nothing stops it; the demand just isn't there"
- **Routing signal, not a warning** — "the tissue bank handles sizing; that's not my problem." The sizing and supply burden is real but sits with the tissue bank and the OR scheduler. Take the question to conversation 4

#### Gap 3 · What the problem costs

*Not found.* No cost per MAT episode, allograft price, or arthroplasty cost attributable to meniscal loss was opened.

- **Ask** — "What does a meniscal transplant cost your institution, graft included, and who approves it? And when a 50-year-old with an old meniscectomy comes in for a knee replacement, does anyone connect the two bills?"
- **Closed if** — a graft price, an episode cost, and a named approver; and a yes or no on whether anyone links the arthroplasty to the earlier loss
- **Warning if** — nobody can name the graft price or the approver, and nobody connects the two

#### Gap 4 · Whether the old and foreign numbers hold here

*Old data and borrowed population.* The 48%-vs-7% figure is from open meniscectomy in 1973; the 13.49% arthroplasty figure is from a UK cohort with a mean age of 50. A US rate for today's young partial-meniscectomy patient was not found.

- **Ask** — "Of the young patients you took significant meniscus out of ten or fifteen years ago, how many have come back with arthritis? How do you know?"
- **Closed if** — they give an estimate and a way they learn of it (follow-up, referrals back, imaging)
- **Warning if** — they believe it is rare in their patients and cannot say how they would know

#### Gap 5 · How often a young patient's tear is irreparable

*Not found.* The entry point to the problem is the tear that cannot be saved; no rate was found.

- **Ask** — "Of the meniscus tears you operated on last month in patients under 40, how many could you repair, and in how many did you end up removing most of the meniscus?"
- **Closed if** — a proportion, and a description of what decides it (tear pattern, location, timing, surgeon skill)
- **Warning if** — "almost everything is repairable now" from all three surgeons

#### Gap 6 · Who owns the downstream arthritis

*Practitioner-only.* The harm arrives a decade or more after the operation, usually in a different surgeon's clinic.

- **Ask** — "When a patient you did a meniscectomy on comes back years later with arthritis, do you hear about it? Does it change anything for you?"
- **Closed if** — the surgeon describes feeling it — reputation, a difficult conversation, referrals — or names who does
- **Warning if** — "they go to the joint-replacement guys; I never see them again," with no consequence to anyone who could have acted
- **Routing signal, not a warning** — the arthroplasty surgeon says "I see them all the time; nobody upstream knows." The cost is real but lands with conversation 3 and the payer. Ask them who should know

#### Gap 7 · What happens when a MAT wears out

*Open.* Survivorship is measured (73.5% at 10 years, 60.3% at 15); what follows a failed or deteriorated graft is not described in the record.

- **Ask** — "The last meniscal transplant of yours that failed or wore down — what happened next for that patient, and what would you have wanted instead?"
- **Closed if** — a specific course (revision transplant, osteotomy, living with it, arthroplasty) and a stated wish
- **Warning if** — failures are described as rare and inconsequential — "they do fine"

#### Gap 8 · Whether the shift to repair is shrinking the problem

*Open.* Repair is rising and debridement falling; whether that is eliminating the meniscus-deficient knee or only trimming it is not measured.

- **Ask** — "Compared with ten years ago, are you seeing more or fewer young patients with significant meniscal loss? What's driving it?"
- **Closed if** — a direction and a reason (repair technique, indications, referral patterns)
- **Warning if** — all five say "fewer every year, and it's going to zero"

### Core questions

1. Tell me about the last young patient whose meniscus you couldn't save. What did you do, and what did you tell them about their knee's future?
2. When did you last perform or refer a meniscal transplant? What decided it, and what got in the way?
3. How do you find out, years later, what happened to the knees you took meniscus out of?
4. What do you wish you had in the room when a tear turns out to be irreparable?
5. Who in your institution notices when a 35-year-old comes back for a knee replacement at 50?

### Disqualifying findings

*Written before the interviews. Any of these should make the founder seriously question the thesis.*

1. Surgeons say the young, symptomatic, meniscus-deficient patient is rare in their practice and cannot recall one from the past year.
2. MAT surgeons say graft supply and sizing are fine and the only limit is that few patients need it.
3. Nobody can describe a consequence of meniscal loss they personally see — the arthritis belongs to someone else, decades later, and nobody connects it back.
4. Everyone says the answer is repair, and that the irreparable tear is vanishing.
5. The payers who fund the arthroplasty do not connect it to the meniscectomy and see no reason to pay for replacement at the time of loss.

### How to run the conversations

- **Do not pitch.** Do not describe, name, or hint at the technology. If asked what you are building, say you are studying the problem first and will share later.
- **Ask about the last time, not the usual case.** Specific past events beat opinions about hypotheticals.
- **Ask what it cost** — in time, money, staff, outcomes — and who paid.
- **"That sounds interesting" means nothing.** A story about a recurring complication with its consequences is a signal.
- **Let silence work.** Do not fill it or lead the witness.
- **Write down their words, not your summary.** Note which gap each answer closes.
- **When someone says a step costs nothing, ask who pays for the people and the time it takes.** A cost the speaker doesn't feel is usually a cost someone else does — find that person before you strike the gap.
- **Five conversations are a screen, not a validation.** They decide whether the idea earns thirty more.

### What to bring back

For each of the eight gaps: **closed**, **still open**, or **contradicted**, with the quote that decided it. Re-run the gate with those notes as input; the verdict, the gaps, and the flags will update.
